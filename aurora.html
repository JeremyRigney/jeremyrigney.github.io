Overhead is NOAA’s chance of aurora straight up, given a clear, dark sky.

On the horizon is the best chance anywhere in sight, out to about 1,000 km, wherever the aurora’s upper edge would stand clear of the horizon. At low Kp the model’s faint edge reaches further than the eye can follow, so the verdict also waits for Kp.

Sky is how dark it is here at the time shown. Cloud, beside the time, is the total cloud cover around here then, from Open-Meteo, which is sent this place rounded to half a degree.

The magnetopause is where the Earth’s field holds the wind off, and the bow shock where the wind, faster than any wave in it, is first slowed. Both are drawn to scale from that wind: the magnetopause by Shue et al. (1998), from its pressure and Bz; the shock’s distance by Farris and Russell (1994), from its Mach number. The distances above are along the line to the Sun, in Earth radii.

The field lines are a sketch: a dipole at today’s tilt, squeezed and stretched to fit inside that magnetopause. The streaks are a sketch of the flow, slowing at the shock and parting round the Earth; their speed and number follow the measured speed and density.

WSA-Enlil is the model NOAA’s forecasters use: it takes the Sun’s magnetic field as mapped that day and carries the wind out past Mars. Drawn here is its radial speed in the plane of the Earth’s orbit, seen from the north, from 0.1 to 1.7 AU. The frame keeps the Earth still, so the Sun’s 27-day rotation sweeps the spiral past it.

The time slider runs the model from a day back to three days on. The Sun and planets are marks, not to scale: at this size the Sun would be a pixel across.

      The solar wind from the Sun is NOAA SWPC’s WSA-Enlil run, reduced for this page whenever a new one is out. Around the Earth, the magnetopause is Shue et al. (1998) and the bow shock’s distance Farris and Russell (1994), from the wind measured at L1; the field lines and the flow round them are sketches. Planets from JPL’s approximate elements.

      Away from live, the time slider shows OVATION-Prime 2010 driven by NOAA’s observed and forecast Kp, as MagIE’s forecast animation is. Each Kp stands in for the median solar-wind coupling seen at that Kp in NASA’s OMNI record, 2012–2025, and the model is matched to NOAA’s hemispheric power near the present. It runs small for storms above about Kp 6, and a Kp forecast more than a day out is itself uncertain. Magnetic coordinates are AACGM-v2.

      Kp runs from 0 to 9: how disturbed the Earth’s field is. From Ireland, aurora usually shows low in the north from about Kp 5.

      Bz is the north–south part of the solar wind’s magnetic field. While it points south (negative), the wind’s energy gets in and the aurora grows.

      Power is NOAA’s estimate of the energy going into the northern aurora.
